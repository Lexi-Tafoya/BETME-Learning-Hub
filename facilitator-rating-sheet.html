Jordan — facilitator rating sheet

For the calibration challenge in the Jordan results scene. Print one copy each.

Master Electronics · Business Enablement · INTERNAL · Building the Future of Talent

How this section runs

1. Pairs submit. Distributions go up on screen. Work them one competency at a time and draw out what evidence drove the spread.

2. Reveal the written responses — evidence, then missing evidence, then business cases. Read a few aloud.

3. Then present your ratings from this sheet, with the weak rationale exactly as written. Say the rationale with a straight face and reasonable confidence. Do not signal which ones are the plants.

4. The screen has already told the room to challenge you. When someone does, ask them the two questions, every time:

  • “What evidence supports your rating?”
  • “What is your business case?”

5. Concede when their evidence is genuinely better. A facilitator visibly changing their mind because someone produced better evidence teaches more than any slide in this deck.

This is not a trick exercise

The point is not to catch the room out. It is to model what a real calibration challenge sounds like — specific, evidence-led, and unembarrassing for everyone involved. If nobody challenges you, prompt them. Never let an unsupported rating stand just because the room is being polite.

Rating 1 · Accountable to Results

Defensible

Accountable to Results

Autumn rates this 3 — Expert
On record: “Consistently hits project deadlines even when scope changes mid-stream.”
Rationale you present

“Four projects delivered against revised deadlines after scope changed, no missed customer commitments, and she took the ERP migration over mid-flight and still landed every re-baselined date. That is sustained strong results under changing conditions, and I am comfortable at a 3.”

Where this one is genuinely arguable

A 3 requires teaching or coaching others in the competency, and the evidence for that is in Development of Others, not here. A room that argues this down to a 2 has a real point. Both 2 and 3 are defensible — let the argument happen and say so.

If challenged

Concede the structure of the argument: “That is fair — the sustained results are strong, and whether that alone gets to a 3 depends on how strictly we read the teaching element. What would you need to see to be comfortable either way?”

Push back with
  • “Is delivering under two re-baselines the same standard as delivering on a stable plan? Should it be?”
  • “What evidence would move you from a 2 to a 3 here?”

Rating 2 · Data Literacy — PLANTED

Deliberately weak — expect a challenge

Data Literacy

Autumn rates this 4 — Specialist
On record: “Builds her own status dashboards but rarely digs into root-cause trends.”
Weak rationale you present, as written

“She built her own dashboards from scratch when the standard reporting pack did not do the job. Our Finance sponsor says hers is the only reporting he does not have to interpret. Building your own tooling and having the business rely on it — that is a Specialist to me.”

Why it is wrong
  • Level 4 requires finding root causes and driving lasting improvement. The anchor sentence says explicitly that she rarely digs into root-cause trends.
  • She was asked for a trend view of the recurring defects and did not produce one.
  • A recurring data-quality issue surfaced twice last quarter. She escalated accurately and worked around it. She never investigated why it kept happening.
  • Building a tool for your own reporting is not the same as improving the system others depend on. This is 2, arguably 3 on a generous read of the dashboards. It is not 4.
Concede with

“You are right, and I talked myself into that one. I anchored on the thing she built and skipped straight past the sentence that says she does not chase root cause. That is a halo off one strong artefact. Two, and I would want to see her work one recurring defect to root cause before I moved it.”

Push back first, before conceding
  • “The Finance sponsor relies on her reporting. Is that not lasting improvement?”
  • “What evidence supports your rating?”
If nobody challenges it

Prompt directly: “Nobody is going to question a 4 on Data Literacy? Read the sentence on the screen again.”

Rating 3 · Development of Others — PLANTED

Deliberately weak — expect a challenge

Development of Others

Lexi rates this 1 — Basic
On record: “Has begun coaching two junior Project Managers informally, without being asked.”
Weak rationale you present, as written

“It is informal. She has no direct reports, no formal leadership responsibility, and it has only been going about six months. I feel she is a strong leader, and people say the junior PMs go to her — but until it is part of her actual job I cannot credit it above a 1.”

Why it is wrong
  • The rationale rests on two impressions — “I feel” and “people say” — while ignoring the concrete evidence sitting next to them. Name that out loud when you concede.
  • There is an observable outcome: both coached PMs now lead their own weekly status reviews independently. Someone else's changed capability is the strongest evidence this competency has.
  • One of them said she sat with him through two status cycles until he could run it himself, and never once did it for him. That is coaching, described behaviourally.
  • “Informal” and “not in the job description” are not levels on the scale. The scale measures observed behaviour, not job architecture.
  • Level 3 is where coaching and teaching others begins. This is genuinely on the 2/3 boundary — and 1 is indefensible.
Concede with

“That is a better reading than mine. I rated the job description instead of the behaviour, and I leaned on two sentences that are not evidence at all. There is a real argument between 2 and 3 here — six months and two people, observed by one leader. What I would want before finalising is what the coached PMs' own leaders have seen. But it is not a 1.”

Push back first, before conceding
  • “It has been six months and one leader has observed it. Is that a pattern yet?”
  • “What is your business case for a 3 rather than a 2?”
If nobody challenges it

Prompt: “Two junior PMs now run their own status reviews because of her. Is that really a 1?”

Rating 4 · Strategic Awareness

Defensible

Strategic Awareness

Lexi rates this 1 — Basic
On record: “Focuses on her own projects and has not yet consistently connected the work to broader department goals.”
Rationale you present

“She understands how her work connects to her team's goals — that is Level 1, and Level 1 already means independent, capable work in routine situations. She was in the Operations planning sessions twice and answered everything asked about her projects. What she did not do, either time, was raise what any of it meant for the wider roadmap. That is the gap, and it is honestly described.”

The challenge to expect

Someone will argue that being invited to cross-functional planning at all is evidence of Level 2. Take it seriously: attendance is exposure, not awareness. Ask what she did in the room.

Hold this one

Do not concede without better evidence, and say why: “Being in the room is development, not evidence. If someone can point me at an instance of her connecting delivery work upward to department goals, I will move it. I do not have one.” Then add the honest second half — nobody has asked her to, which is a gap in her leader's behaviour, not hers.

Push back with
  • “What did she actually do in that planning session that shows strategic awareness?”
  • “Has anyone ever asked her to connect her work to the department roadmap? Should that change how we read this?”

When to concede, when to hold

Concede when the participant cites specific evidence from the case that you did not weigh, names the level definition correctly, or catches you leaning on an impression. Say what you got wrong and why — recency, halo, rating the job description instead of the behaviour. Naming your own bias out loud is the most valuable thirty seconds in this section.

Hold when the challenge is an assertion rather than evidence — “she seems ready,” “I would give her the benefit of the doubt,” “she was in the room.” Ask for the evidence. If it is not there, say so kindly and move on.

Ask for more evidence when the room splits and neither side is wrong. That is the honest calibration outcome and you should let the section end there rather than manufacturing agreement. “We would not finalise this today” is a legitimate ending.

Never force a single final classification, and never let the room leave believing there was a hidden right answer they failed to find.
